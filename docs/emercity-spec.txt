  1. Product overview
  Emercity is a cross-platform household emergency app that knows who in your home is
  most at risk, for this hazard, at this address, right now, and tells you what to do first. It is
  framed for Loudoun County, Virginia, and uses real Loudoun open data where it exists,
  with patterns modeled on data-rich counties (Fairfax County, VA and Harris County, TX)
  and national sources (FEMA, NOAA, DOE, HHS, FDA) where Loudoun is thin.

  This document is the complete build specification for the app only. It covers every screen,
  component, rule, data field, and state. It is written so it can be handed to a developer or
  another AI chat and built in one pass.


  1.1 One-line pitch
  Emercity turns a household's people, pets, medications, and address into a ranked, step-
  by-step plan that adapts to the hazard in front of them, and works with no signal.


  1.2 The problem
        Generic preparedness templates (FEMA Make-a-Plan, Red Cross) treat every
        household the same. They do not know that one family member depends on an oxygen
        concentrator and another on refrigerated insulin.
        Official alerts tell you what is happening, not what your specific household should do
        about it, in what order.
        In a real emergency, people freeze, forget, and lose signal. Paper plans are forgotten in
        drawers.
        Loudoun County sees frequent power outages, flash flooding along creeks, winter
        storms, and inland effects of hurricanes. Each hazard changes who is most at risk.

  1.4 Product principles
  These rules govern every design and engineering decision. When a decision is unclear,
  apply them in this order.

    1. Safety over cleverness. Every recommendation must trace to a written rule and a
       cited source. No feature that drives an emergency action depends on AI output.
    2. Works with no signal. Everything needed during an emergency (the plan, the priority
       order, the guides, the countdowns, the lock-screen card) is stored on the device and
       works in airplane mode.
    3. Order, not essays. In emergencies, the app shows what to do next, in order, one step at
       a time. Explanations live one tap away, never in the way.
    4. Life-threatening first. Anything that could be fatal if no action is taken is visually
       louder than everything else, always.
    5. Honest data. Every live or location-based value shows its source and a "last updated"
       time. Stale data is labeled stale. The app never pretends to know what it does not.
    6. Private by default. Medical details are encrypted, visible only to household members,
       and never sold or used for anything but the plan.

    7. Never replace 911 or official orders. The app always defers to official evacuation
       orders and emergency services, and makes calling 911 one tap away in Emergency
        Mode.


  1.5 Scope
  In scope for version 1:

        Household profiles (people, pets, medications, medical devices)
        Priority engine (rule-based, ranked order with severity nudges)
        Address risk profile
        Live official alerts and nearest river gauge watch
        Outage countdown for medications, devices, and food
        Emergency Mode with hazard-specific playbooks (flood, hurricane inland effects,
        winter storm and outage, fire, chemical/hazmat)
        Medication barcode scan via openFDA
        Lock-screen card generator
        Practice drills
        Map: shelters, resources, meeting points
        Supply tracker and seasonal nudges
        Offline guide library, plus a subtle cloud Q&A restricted to official sources
        Shared household plan with accounts and cloud sync
        Multilingual guides and accessibility

  Out of scope for version 1 (listed as future work): car-specific plans, plans for when you
  are away from home, school pickup coordination, neighbor check-ins, livestock,
  smartwatch app.

  2. Design system
  The visual language is calm in normal use and unmistakably loud in an emergency. Two
  modes share one system: Prepare (light, calm, navy and teal) and Emergency (dark, high-
  contrast, severity colors dominate).

  dark. Hex values are starting points; every text/background pair must pass WCAG AA
  contrast (4.5:1 for body text, 3:1 for large text and icons).

  Rules:

        Severity colors are never used for decoration. Red means "could be fatal" and nothing
        else.
        Color never carries meaning alone. Every severity also has an icon and a text label.
        Emergency Mode always uses the dark palette regardless of system setting, because it
        reads better at night and saves battery on OLED screens.

  2.3 Typography
        Font: system fonts (SF Pro on iOS, Roboto on Android) for legibility and Dynamic Type
        support. Numbers in countdowns use tabular figures so digits do not jump as they tick.
        Scale (Prepare mode, default size): Display 34/41 bold, Title 1 28/34 bold, Title 2 22/28
        semibold, Headline 17/22 semibold, Body 17/24 regular, Callout 16/21, Subhead 15/20,
        Footnote 13/18, Caption 12/16.
        Emergency Mode bumps every size up one step and never goes below 17 pt for
        actionable text.
        All text supports Dynamic Type up to the largest accessibility size. Layouts must reflow
        (stack vertically) rather than truncate.


  2.4 Spacing, shape, and layout
        4 pt base grid; standard spacing steps 4, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32, 48.
        Screen side margin 16 pt on phones, 24 pt on tablets.
        Card corner radius 16 pt; buttons 12 pt; chips fully rounded.
        Minimum touch target 48 × 48 pt everywhere, 64 pt tall for primary actions in
        Emergency Mode.
        Maximum content width 640 pt on tablets (centered).

  2.6 Iconography
        Use one consistent outline icon set (SF Symbols on iOS, Material Symbols on Android,
        or a single cross-platform set such as Phosphor or Lucide).




                                                                                                                 Page 8 of 105
Emercity — Full App Layout & Feature Specification




         Each hazard has one fixed icon: water waves (flood), snowflake (winter storm),
         lightning bolt with slash (outage), flame (fire), hazard diamond (chemical/hazmat),
         spiral (hurricane).
         Members: person silhouette; pets: paw; medication: pill; device: plug; supplies: box;
         shelter: house with heart; hospital: cross; fire station: helmet; meeting point: flag.


  2.7 Motion
         Default transitions 200–250 ms ease-out.
         Emergency Mode entry: 400 ms cross-fade to the dark palette with a brief hazard-
         color sweep, so the user clearly knows the mode has changed.
         Critical cards pulse slowly (section 2.2). All motion respects the system Reduce
         Motion setting.


  2.8 Voice and tone of copy
         Prepare mode: friendly, plain, encouraging ("You're 72% ready. Two things left.").
         Emergency Mode: short imperative sentences, verb first, under 10 words where
         possible ("Move insulin to the cooler now.").
         Never alarmist adjectives. Severity is carried by the tier treatment, not by words like
         "terrifying" or "deadly."
         Reading level: grade 6 or below for all Emergency Mode text.

  4. Data model
  All household data lives in one shared household document, synced to the cloud and
  mirrored in an encrypted local database on each member's phone. Reference data
  (guides, medication storage rules, risk layers) is cached separately and is read-only.

  8. Priority engine
  The priority engine ranks every person and pet in the household for each hazard, assigns
  each one a severity tier, and outputs an ordered list. It is fully rule-based and
  deterministic: the same inputs always produce the same order, and every placement
  traces to a written rule. This is the headline feature.

  Per the product decision, the list shows order only. No reasons are printed on the cards.
  Instead, the tier treatment (section 2.2) visually pushes life-threatening subjects forward:
  Critical subjects pulse in red, sit inside a red "Act on these first" group, and trigger a
  haptic the first time they appear in Emergency Mode.

  8.3 Algorithm
    1. For the selected hazard, evaluate every tier rule (section 8.4) against each subject. A
        subject's tier is the highest tier any rule assigns it. Subjects matching no rule get
        Standard.
    2. Compute a within-tier score (section 8.5).
    3. Sort by tier (Critical → Urgent → Important → Standard), then score descending, then
        tie-breakers: needs help evacuating first, then youngest child, then oldest adult, then
        alphabetical by first name.
    4. Pets: within any tier, all humans come before all pets. Pets can never exceed Important.
    5. Apply user pins (section 8.8) last, subject to the Critical lock.

    6. Assign ranks 1..N.

  9.3 Per-hazard risk scores

        Winter storm: base 50 + electric heat dependence +15 + rural/long driveway flag (user)
        +10 + outage frequency High +10.
        Flood: base 25 + flood zone (High +45, Moderate +20) + stream distance (High +20,
        Moderate +10) + basement +10.
        Fire: base 30 (house fire applies to everyone) + oxygen in home +20 + mobility-limited
        member +10 + no smoke alarm confirmed (from supply checklist) +20.
        Chemical/hazmat: base 10 + rail (High +30, Moderate +15) + hazmat facility (High +30,
        Moderate +15) + highway under 0.5 mi +10.
        Hurricane (inland): base 25 + flood score × 0.4 + outage score × 0.3 + mobile home

  11. Outage countdown
  The outage countdown shows, for every power-dependent item in the home, how long it
  stays safe without electricity, as a live timer. It covers three kinds of items: refrigerated
  medications, electricity-dependent medical devices, and refrigerated/frozen food. Every

  11.3 Countdown math
  Refrigerated medication, still in the fridge:

        Phase 1 (fridge holding): 4 hours from outage start, reduced by 30 minutes each time
        the user logs "opened fridge".
        Phase 2 (room temperature): after phase 1, the product's room-temperature limit
        applies. If the indoor temperature (user-entered, or estimated from outdoor
        temperature when heating/cooling is out) exceeds the product's max, the countdown
        switches to "Unsafe — move to cooler now" immediately.
        The tile shows the phase 1 remainder prominently, because acting during phase 1
        (moving to a cooler) is the most important action.

  Moved to cooler: user picks cooler size (small, medium, large) and cold source (ice packs,
  regular ice, dry ice). Default holding times are conservative estimates shown as ranges,
  user-editable, with the warning "Never let insulin touch ice directly. Wrap it in a towel."

  Medical device:

     T_{backup} = T_{internal} + n_{spare} \cdot T_{spare} + T_{generator} +
     T_{nonelectric}


  where each term is the user-entered runtime and the generator term applies only if
  generator watts cover the device. The tile counts down from this total and shows the
  current stage ("Internal battery", "Spare battery 1 of 2", "Oxygen cylinder") with a button
  "Switched to next backup" so the user can keep the stage accurate.

  Food: fixed timers, with a toggle on the freezer tile for "full" vs "half full".

  12.5 How playbooks are built
  A playbook is generated at runtime from three layers:

    1. Hazard template: fixed ordered steps for the hazard (12.8), each with an ID, tier, and
        conditions for inclusion.
    2. Subject steps: for each Critical and Urgent subject in the priority order, the template
       inserts that subject's specific steps right after the hazard's "immediate safety" step, in
        rank order. For example, in an outage: "Check Grandma's oxygen battery" → "Switch
        Grandma to backup when below 30 min", then "Move Dad's insulin to the cooler".
    3. Household conditions: steps included or removed based on the household (no
        basement → skip "Leave the basement"; no pets → skip pet steps; generator → add
        "Start generator outside, 20+ ft from windows").

  Ordering rule: life-safety steps (get out, take shelter, call 911) always come first; then
  Critical subject steps in rank order; then Urgent subject steps; then general protective
  steps; then comfort and recovery.

  13. Medication barcode scan
  The user points the camera at the barcode on a medication box, Emercity extracts the
  National Drug Code (NDC), looks it up in the FDA's public NDC Directory through
  openFDA, and pre-fills the medication with name, strength, form, and the matching
  storage rule. It is deterministic (no AI or OCR guessing) and is the best single "wow"
  moment in a demo.


  13.1 Flow
    1. HH-06 → "Scan barcode" (or from onboarding ONB-08, or the member profile).
    2. HH-07 camera screen opens.
    3. On-device barcode detection reads the code (UPC-A, EAN-13, GS1 DataBar, GS1
       DataMatrix).
    4. The app extracts a candidate NDC (13.3).
    5. Lookup: first the bundled offline NDC index (top refrigerated and critical medications,
        ~5–10 MB), then openFDA if online.
    6. HH-08 confirmation screen shows the match.
    7. User picks the owner, confirms dose, opened/unopened, and quantity. Saved.


  13.2 HH-07 Camera screen layout
        Full-screen camera preview with a rounded rectangle guide in the center ("Line up the
        barcode on the box").
        Torch toggle (top right), close X (top left).
        Below the guide: a hint that changes after 5 seconds without a read: "Try the barcode
        on the outer box, not the pharmacy sticker."
        Detected code flashes green with a light haptic, then the screen transitions
        automatically.
        Bottom: "Type it instead" link (opens search) and "Enter NDC number" (manual 10- or
        11-digit entry with format helper).




                                                                                            Page 64 of 105
Emercity — Full App Layout & Feature Specification




  13.3 Extracting the NDC
        UPC-A / EAN-13 on U.S. drug packaging: the NDC is embedded in the 12-digit UPC
        (number system 3). Strip the leading "3" and the check digit to get the 10-digit NDC.
        GS1 DataMatrix / DataBar (newer packaging): parse the GTIN (AI 01), then derive the
        NDC from the GTIN the same way. Also read expiration date (AI 17) and lot (AI 10) when
        present and pre-fill expiration.
        10-digit NDC ambiguity: a 10-digit NDC can be in 4-4-2, 5-3-2, or 5-4-1 format without
        dashes. The app tries all three hyphenations against the directory and keeps the one
        that matches a listed package.
        Pharmacy labels: many prescription bottle stickers carry the pharmacy's internal
        barcode, not an NDC. If no NDC can be derived, show "This looks like a pharmacy
        barcode. Scan the manufacturer's box, or type the medicine name."


  13.4 openFDA lookup
        Endpoint: the openFDA drug NDC endpoint ( api.fda.gov/drug/ndc.json ) searching
        by product_ndc or packaging.package_ndc .
        Fields used: brand_name, generic_name, labeler_name, dosage_form, route,
        active_ingredients (name + strength), packaging description, product_type.
        Rate limits: openFDA works without a key at up to 240 requests per minute and 1,000
        per day per IP; a free API key raises the daily limit to 120,000 (openFDA usage notes).
        Requests go through the Emercity server with its key and a 30-day cache, so individual
        phones never hit the limit.
        openFDA data is public domain.
        Note shown in the confirmation: the directory lists what the manufacturer submitted;
        FDA does not verify each entry, so the user should confirm the name matches the box.

  14. Lock-screen card generator (PLAN-09)
  The lock-screen card is a generated wallpaper image that puts the household's most
  critical information on the phone's lock screen, readable without unlocking: meeting
  point, key contacts, and the top critical needs. Each member can make their own version.
  It is the only output the user chose, and it works on every phone with no app open.


  14.1 Why a wallpaper

  20. Multilingual support and accessibility
  Emercity must work for every member of the household, including people who don't read
  English well and people with disabilities, because they are often the ones most at risk.
  Language and accessibility are built into every component, not added later.


  20.1 Languages
        Launch set: English and Spanish fully translated (UI, playbooks, guides, lock-screen
        card). Additional languages are chosen from the most-spoken non-English languages in
        Loudoun County according to Census American Community Survey data (verify
        before release; likely candidates include Korean, Vietnamese, Chinese, Hindi, Urdu,
        Arabic, Telugu, and Amharic).
        Per-person language: each Member has a primary language. Each account holder's app
        displays in their language. Lock-screen cards and printed plans can be bilingual
        (household language + member's language).
        Safety text is never machine-translated. Playbook steps, guides, and countdown
        messages ship as human-translated strings reviewed by a fluent speaker. Untranslated
        items fall back to English with a note, never to machine translation.
        Official alerts: NWS text may be English only. The app offers "Translate" clearly labeled
        "Machine translation — the original is below," and shows the mapped hazard and the
        household's own playbook (which is professionally translated) above it.

        returns any conflicts for display.
        The OfflineBar shows "Offline · 3 changes waiting to sync" and, after reconnecting,
        "Synced" for 3 seconds.

  25.4 Edge cases
        Two hazards at once (e.g., winter storm plus outage, or flood plus outage): Emergency
        Mode supports multiple active hazards; the priority order merges by taking each
        subject's highest tier across active hazards, and the playbook interleaves life-safety
        steps from both first.
        Member away from home during an emergency: check-in statuses cover this; steps
        assigned to an absent member auto-suggest reassignment ("Dad is away. Reassign
        'Get Grandma' to Mom?").
        Traveling: alerts follow the home address; with Always location on, the user can also
        get alerts for their current location (labeled "Where you are" vs "At home").
        Multiple households: Emergency Mode is per household; the hazard picker asks which
        household if the user belongs to more than one and alerts affect both.
        Household with no adults signed in (e.g., teen alone): Emergency Mode works fully;
        "End for everyone" requires an adult; teen sees an extra "Call a parent" button.
        Device clock wrong: countdowns use monotonic elapsed time from the start event,
        not wall-clock math, so a time-zone or clock change doesn't break them.
        App killed during an outage: countdown notifications are pre-scheduled locally, so
        they still fire; reopening the app restores state from the database.
        Phone dies: another member's phone holds the same plan; the lock-screen card and
        printed plan are the fallback.

          Medication with no storage rule: never guessed; user enters label values (section 11.2).
